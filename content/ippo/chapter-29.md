---
title: "Chapter 29"
---

Light burned Ippo's eyes.

He had clenched them shut against the sudden boom of it — the shift from what had been darkness to what was now a white wall pressing against his eyelids. The first bursts hit his pupils and made a humming noise, or the humming was already there and the light gave it a shape, a frequency that sat behind his forehead like a tuning fork struck against bone. The bright panels above the ring forced him to obey the burning sensation, his body wanting to lift an arm for coverage, but the arm was nailed to the floor. Not held. Not pinned. Just nailed — the weight of it so complete that the instruction traveling from his brain to his shoulder arrived and found the door locked.

The blurred existence of his limbs grew steadily heavier. His arm began to vibrate — a weak, involuntary tremor that started in the bicep and traveled down to the wrist. The more he gave in to the burning light, the more the vibration increased. The more he tried to lift against it, the worse his muscles burned, a second fire layered on top of the first, until the whole system quit. His arm stopped trying. His body surrendered the pursuit.

The light eased into his eyes the way water fills a glass — not all at once, but in a rising line that displaced the darkness slowly from the bottom up. As the pain settled, a vision of Miyata appeared.

Not a memory. Not a thought. A direction — the angle his eyes had been pointing when the hook landed, the last image his brain had saved before the lights went out. The face was blurred and it was already fading but it was there the way a sunspot is there after you look away from the source, burned into the dark.

Ippo's skin went cold. The burning from the shock drained out of him and what replaced it was a chill that began at the surface and worked inward. He could feel the hairs on his arms standing straight, each one a tiny needle pointing away from his body, sending the cold down through his skin and into the tissue beneath it. The chill reached his chest. It reached his heart. He gasped — the gasp of something dead rendered alive, the lungs expanding against ribs that had forgotten they were supposed to move — and into that cold heart he swallowed the voice of the hands shaking him left and right, screaming his name.

---

The voice was harsh. It felt like pinches of needle on his forehead — each syllable a small sharp point pressed into the skin above his eyes. His body was being moved left and right and he wanted to push back against it, to make it stop, but he was too deep in the space between conscious and not to resist. His lips gathered together. His soul moved like gelatin inside a body that was still but followed the motion anyway, the way a yolk moves inside a shell when you shake it.

A smell hit his nose. Old leather. Liniment. The kind of smell that lives in the creases of hands that have spent decades gripping rope and tape and the wrists of young men who didn't know how to wrap themselves. His mouth pushed toward his nose — an involuntary scrunch, the face trying to scratch an itch it couldn't reach. The voice turned from a movement into an echo, a sound that traveled the length of his skull and ruptured through the image of Miyata into something familiar.

The pressure of those hands — full of wrinkles, the calluses rough against his shoulders — left a mark he knew. The same mark. The one that was always there when those hands wrapped his fists, every single time, for thousands of times, and it was always missed until now. The specific pressure of fingers that had wound tape across his knuckles in loops so practiced they had become a kind of handwriting — a signature pressed into his skin before every fight, every spar, every morning when his hands became instruments and the wraps became the preparation.

He knew it was Kamogawa. But he shifted his eyes to the left anyway. Moved his head to confirm.

Every echo of the coach's voice sounded like it had passed through a pool of water before reaching him. Every glimpse of the old man's face made Ippo's eyes close — on and off, on and off — the lids too heavy to hold, especially against that muffled echo that sounded like gibberish shaped into the outline of his name.

Then a voice like wrath struck his ears.

His eyes opened fully. The world arranged itself around him — the ring, the ropes, the ceiling beams, the faces at the edge of the canvas — and with the world came the knowledge that Miyata's presence was still in the room and the coach's screams were the screams of his name.

The coach was saying Ippo. Ippo.

---

He was forced to sit up. The world around him told a story that made his stomach clench. The sound of stillness hurt more than the rubbery feeling in his mouth — that numb, swollen sensation of lips and tongue that had been compressed by a mouthguard and then released, coupled with a dryness so complete he couldn't swallow correctly. His throat worked against nothing. The muscles contracted and found no moisture and the effort sent a small spike of pain down below his jaw.

Eyes landed on his skin. He could feel them — not see them, feel them — the way you feel heat from a source you haven't turned to face. The gazes pressed against his sides like something physical, needles entering through his ribs, the sensation settling deep in his intestines. He became slowly conscious that all of his body was reporting in, one system at a time, and every system was accounted for except one.

His jaw.

His jaw was screaming. The left side — the side where the hook had landed — sent a sharp bright pain through his face when he tried to move it, tried to swallow, tried to close his mouth against the rubbery metallic taste that had filled it. Metal. Where had the metal come from? He gave the question one moment — one single moment of wondering — before a sharper pain hit the side of his face like a second punch and he struggled to lift his arm. The glove touched the spot. The padding pressed against the swelling. And Ippo's eyes went wide at the presence of those gloves on his hands.

The gloves were heavy. They sat on the ends of his arms like objects that belonged to someone else — the weight of them speaking words that no one in the gym could translate, not even Ippo. Kamogawa glimpsed his face and moved him again, tilting him forward, pouring water down his mouth while icing the jaw with his other hand. The water was cold and the ice was colder and between them Ippo's teeth ached.

Now all of his body was chills. All except his back. His back was pressed against a fiber-like texture — the ropes. The kind of material that if you lean against it too loosely and too long it bites into the skin and leaves a welt. The thought of the welt brought the thought of blood and the thought of blood brought the metallic taste back into his mouth, mixing with the water that was pooled there, and before he could follow that chain to its conclusion his eyes were captured by the light of the ring.

The ring looked bigger from down here. It had looked bigger when his eyes were first opening — the ropes wider apart, the canvas stretching further in every direction — but the more conscious he became the more the ring shrank. The ropes on the far side seemed to lean inward. The corners pressed closer. The space between where he sat and where the canvas bore his mark — the smudge of sweat and skin where his body had landed — was shorter than it should have been. He was sitting next to his own outline. The drops of blood beside it were small and dark and they were his.

His eyes searched the room. Everything felt closer than it was — the heavy bag, the wall clock, the bodies standing at the ring's edge. But he was searching past all of it, searching for a specific shape in the light, and the window was there, and its light gave Ippo a question.

---

His mouth was full of water. He swallowed it down, feeling the pain travel through his throat and across the iced jaw, and he tried to speak. The word failed. It came out as a shape without sound — his lips forming the syllables, his throat refusing to carry them. Kamogawa noticed. The old man turned back to him, paying attention with eyes that had the careful stillness of someone waiting for a patient to say the thing the doctor already knows.

Ippo asked where Miyata was.

The ring changed when he said it. The ropes on the far side — the ones that had been leaning in — now felt like they were marching toward him, closing the gap between the corners, the whole structure tightening around him like a fist. The light faded dimly. The echo of his own words struck something inside his chest and what came back was the memory of the darkness — the darkness he had traveled through on his way to the floor, the darkness his eyes had been swimming in before the light burned them open. The sound of the floor hit him like a drum — not the sound of now, but the sound of then, the thud of his own body arriving against canvas, replayed inside his skull.

His voice dropped. He said _oh._

Kamogawa's face shifted — the assumption forming behind the old man's eyes that the sound was about Miyata, that the _oh_ was the weight of learning that Miyata had watched him fall. But Ippo lowered his gaze. His head dipped. He looked down at the canvas — the mark he had drawn with his own back, the outline he had been pushed away from when they sat him up. The chills that had been running through him grew weaker. They were replaced by something heavier: numbness. A slow, spreading absence of sensation that started in his fingers and moved inward, filling the spaces the chills had left behind. His arms crossed over his chest. He held himself.

The sweat on his skin had turned cold. Each drop that had been hot during the spar — hot from effort, from the furnace of a body working at its limit — was now a cold point on his surface, a tiny betrayal, the body's moisture turning against its host. He wanted to put on his clothes. He wanted fabric between himself and the air. But he didn't want to walk. He didn't want to stand. His heart ached — not with pain but with a kind of expansion, the organ swelling outward and finding nothing to push against, opening a cavity inside his chest that was empty and growing emptier and the emptiness frightened him.

The gloves. The gloves were the weight. The heaviness on his hands was no longer the weight of leather and padding — it was the weight of what the gloves meant, what they had failed to do, what they had been pointing at when they should have been pointing at Imai. He needed them off.

He asked the coach to remove the weight.

---

Kamogawa spoke as he worked. His hands lifted Ippo's arm — holding it up and out, away from the body, the way he always held it during the unlacing. The old man's fingers worked the laces with the same efficient motions he had used ten thousand times. He told Ippo that Miyata had left. That he had been here a few seconds. That he had watched, and then he had gone.

The first glove came off.

Ippo's hand — bare now except for the wraps — clenched into a fist. The fist moved to his side and hit the floor of the ring. Not hard. A dim, muffled thud — the sound of a hand that wanted to hit something but had forgotten how to commit to the impact. The fist sat against the canvas. The ropes pressed into his back behind him.

Then the fist stopped.

The fingers uncurled. The hand went flat against the floor. No movement. Ippo looked at Kamogawa and his mouth opened and what came out was the thing his body had been building toward since the chills started, since the numbness replaced the chills, since the emptiness opened in his chest and demanded to be filled with something — anything — even blame.

_Sorry, coach._

Kamogawa's face shifted. Surprise — not at the words, but at the speed of them, the way they arrived before the second glove was even off, as if Ippo had been holding them in his mouth longer than the water.

Then the coach said nothing.

He looked at Ippo with a face that was serious the way stone is serious — not performing seriousness but made of it, the expression so still it could have been carved. And Ippo talked. He talked about fighting for the wrong reason. About thinking he could take on someone like Imai without training, not one bit, after this vacation of rest. Kamogawa's eyes held him and the weight of them pushed Ippo's shoulders down, his arms crossing tighter, and still he kept talking — about how he should have trained more strategically, the way coach had said, and it wasn't coach's fault, it was his fault, _my_ fault coach, not yours, you guided me and I didn't obey that guidance.

The words poured out of him like water from a cracked vessel — not because they were true but because they were available. They were the words a boxer is supposed to say after a loss. They were the noble excuse: _I didn't prepare enough. I was undisciplined. The failure is technical and fixable._ They were the lie that protects the deeper wound — the wound that has nothing to do with training volume or strategy or discipline and everything to do with the direction his eyes were pointing when the hook landed.

Kamogawa listened. Said nothing. When Ippo finished, the old man reached for his hand — the wrapped hand, the one still carrying the tape across the knuckles — and began to unwrap it.

Ippo screamed _no._

---

The whole gym stopped.

The voice that had been quiet — the voice of the man who sat on the canvas and apologized and blamed himself in sentences that got softer as they went — that voice hit the room like a hand hitting a table. Heads turned. Conversations broke mid-word. The crowd at the edge of the ring rotated slowly toward the source, each face arriving at Ippo one by one, and the weight of their eyes landed on his sides like knives.

Pressure mounted. The gazes stacked on top of him — each pair of eyes adding a layer of weight to the air above his shoulders. But he breathed into it. He looked at the canvas — at the mark, at the outline, at the small dark drops that were drying — and then he looked at his wrapped hand.

The hand that Kamogawa had tried to strip.

He stared at it. The wraps were still there — the white tape circling his knuckles, crossing his palm, looping his wrist in the pattern that Kamogawa's hands had written onto his hands before every fight since the beginning. The fabric sat against his skin with a pressure he could feel on every finger, every joint, the slight compression of tape holding his bones in formation.

He kept staring. He wanted to stare longer. Because the pressure of the fabric on his fingers and palm — the gentle constant squeeze of the wraps doing their job — was the last thing. The last connection. The gloves were off and the fight was over and Miyata was gone and his jaw was swelling and his chest was empty and the ring was shrinking around him and everything that made him a boxer had been stripped away piece by piece since the hook landed, and the wraps were the last piece. The wraps were the final layer between Ippo and the man underneath the boxer — the man who wasn't training, who wasn't fighting, who had stood in the ring with his eyes on a window instead of his opponent.

Getting rid of the wraps meant seeing his hands. And his hands were clean. His knuckles didn't hurt. The skin wasn't split. The bones weren't swollen. He had stood in the ring with the Japanese featherweight champion's name attached to his and his hands looked like they had spent the afternoon resting on a desk.

He wasn't ready to see that.

He kept the wraps on. He held his hand against his chest — the wrapped hand pressed to the empty space where his heart was trying to fill a cavity it couldn't reach — and he sat on the canvas next to his own outline and he didn't look up.
