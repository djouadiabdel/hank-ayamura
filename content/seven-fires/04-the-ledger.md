<span class="sf-numeral">IV</span>
<h1>Mine Went Down</h1>
</div>
<p class="sf-plate-credit">Buonamico Buffalmacco, <em>The Last Judgement</em>, Camposanto, Pisa, c. 1340</p>
</div>

On the third floor they are given whatever they ask for, at once, and so they count.

The room is full of scales. No two are built alike, but every one is accurate. People move between them carrying portions of food, hours of attention, places at tables, touches, apologies, inheritances, invitations, all the divisible substances of a life. They weigh what they received and set it against what the next person received. The arithmetic is never wrong and never comes out even.

A man took my wrist as we entered and placed my hand on one pan of his scale. On the other was a hand I could not see.

Mine went down.

He smiled as if I had confessed something.

I asked what he believed the scale proved. He said it proved that what he had given was heavier. I pointed out that my hand was not what he had given and the invisible hand was not what he had received. He agreed at once, altered the headings in his book, and performed the same weighing again.

Mine went down.

He smiled in exactly the same way.

They will explain at length and with documents that they are owed. The documents are excellent. The figures can be checked.

I had brought my own ledger. It was bound too well and heavy enough that I carried it against my hip. I had recorded what I gave, what was asked, what arrived unasked, what I had been willing to do, and what willingness ought to count for when the act itself could not be completed. I had separate columns for intention and result. This was fairer, I thought, because results are frequently outside a man's control.

The people nearest us saw the book and assumed I held office there.

Two came forward with a scale between them. One pan held years. The other held a single night. The years were bundled like reeds, each cut to the same length and tied with black thread. The night lay in a glass vessel and moved when nobody touched it.

The first person said he had remained for all the years.

The second said that on the night in the vessel, remaining had become necessary and he had not.

They wanted to know which was heavier.

I told them duration and necessity were different measures and should not share a scale. The first agreed immediately. The second asked whether a thing ceased to be owed merely because it could not be converted.

I said nothing was owed. This floor's entire error was the language of owing.

Then what are you carrying, the second asked.

The ledger had become heavier against my hip. I placed it on a nearby desk and opened to the section on presence. There were subdivisions for willingness, capacity, warning, cost, and whether the person receiving the act had understood the cost correctly. The people gathered close enough that their shoulders touched.

I worked carefully. I removed from the years everything done from habit, everything done while expecting recognition, and everything later used as evidence. The bundle became smaller but did not vanish. From the night I removed fear, confusion, and every consequence the absent person could not reasonably have predicted. The night became lighter too.

We returned both to the scale.

The years went down.

The first person began to cry. The second looked unsurprised.

I explained that the result did not declare anybody good. It established only which contribution carried greater weight under the categories available. The first thanked me. The second picked up the glass vessel.

I did not ask for the years, he said.

He poured the night over them.

The reeds blackened and bent. The scale struck the floor hard enough to break one of its legs.

The people around me began speaking at once. Some said the new fact required a new column. Others said a gift could not be charged against the recipient merely because it had been costly to give. A woman tore a page from my ledger and wrote the sentence down before I could decide whether it was correct.

More scales arrived.

For a while I judged them.

I was good at it. I could find the concealed premise in a complaint before the speaker reached it. I could tell which quantity had been counted twice and which had been left out because including it would ruin the desired sum. People wrote my distinctions in the margins of their books. The line before me reached the far wall.

You stood outside it.

When I finally looked toward you, you had your body turned toward the gate. The line continued moving and I had already raised one hand to call the next case.

I lowered it.

The same hand went to my chest, hip, mouth, shoulder. You moved before it reached my throat.

You let me pass between the scales instead of walking beside me. When the aisle narrowed, you stopped altogether and waited for me to notice.

I did notice. I explained that the ledger was wide.

At the gate an old hand came through the bars and rested on the cover. The sleeve was white. I could not see the man attached to it.

He asked me what I thought was being paid off and to whom.

I said I did not know. That was rather the point. Somebody must be keeping the account.

The hand opened the ledger near the middle. I disliked that. The argument began at the first page and was cumulative, and a person entering halfway could make it say anything.

He read one line.

Then he asked what payment would turn that line into a man who had not written it.

I said payment was not meant to change the past.

What is it meant to change?

The balance.

Where is the balance kept?

I had answered this already. I began again, more carefully. The speech came with its examples in the correct order and the objection already answered at the end of every paragraph. The hand remained on the page. You watched the hand and not me.

When I finished, the man behind the bars said that if a debt could be settled here, a person could leave unchanged with a receipt, and the receipt would be worth exactly what he was, which was nothing yet.

You do not settle up. You are changed, or you are not.

I wish that sentence were mine.

I asked the man behind the bars whether it was his.

No.

Whose, then?

He removed his hand from the ledger. The white sleeve remained visible through the bars.

I said attribution mattered. A sentence detached from its speaker can be made to serve anything, and a person ought to know what life had authorised the words.

Would the sentence become less true in a worse mouth?

No, I said. It would become more dangerous.

Would it become more true in a better one?

No.

Then you are not asking about the sentence.

I was asking who had the right to teach it.

The sleeve shifted. For a moment I saw the outline of a shoulder behind the bars and the fall of white cloth from it. I imagined the face above it before the dark supplied one: old, tired correctly, patient with the errors of people who had not yet read enough.

The man asked whether a right to teach could be entered into my ledger.

I said the question confused authority with quantity.

He said that was the first true thing I had told him.

The third gate took the ledger.

It did not take the pages people had torn out.

Behind us, the line continued. The woman who had copied the sentence about costly gifts was using it to decide another case. She quoted me accurately. The people around her wrote it down beneath their own headings.

I told the old hand that the payment was incomplete while the teaching remained in circulation.

The voice behind the bars asked whether I wanted the sentence removed from them.

I said I wanted no ownership of it.

That was not the question.

The woman began explaining the distinction to somebody who had not heard me. She had improved the example.

I could have gone back and demanded the page. I could have corrected the use, clarified that the sentence described a limit and was not itself a method of judgment. The line made space for me when I turned.

You were already on the far side of the gate.

I said the truth did not become mine merely because I had expressed it first.

The hand withdrew with the ledger.

It opened inward. I stepped back sharply because I had expected it to rise.

There was no reason to expect that. The first had risen. The second had opened inside the wall. I resented the needless inconsistency of the design for several steps.

Without the book, my hands kept closing on nothing. I checked my pockets eleven times on one flight of stairs, and I know it was eleven.

The only thing still on me was on my head. I put my hand up in the dark. It gave a little under the fingers and came back to where it was, and I decided that was the heat.
