<span class="sf-numeral">III</span>
<h1>Anything Freely Given</h1>
</div>
<p class="sf-plate-credit">Fra Angelico, <em>The Last Judgement</em>, c. 1431</p>
</div>

On the second floor I suffer.

Everyone in it is generous. I want that said before we enter, because everything after it will sound like an accusation and it is not one.

The room is laid out like a house after guests have arrived: chairs drawn close, places cleared beside people, cups already poured. There is no kitchen. Nothing is carried through a door. You sit, and whatever you ask for arrives in the chest.

I sat because the room had prepared a chair for me. You remained standing. I greeted the woman across from us, and she asked what I needed.

I said, anything freely given.

It came at once. The chest filled and became a chest again. The shoulders lowered. The mouth stopped preparing its next defence. For a moment I could feel the exact edges of my body and did not need anybody to prove that they were there.

Then it drained.

The woman asked again. She was not tired of asking. Nobody on that floor is ever tired of giving. I answered, and it came again, warm and complete, and drained by the same opening the first gift had failed to close.

I changed the request. General gifts, I explained, fail because they do not know the shape of the lack. I asked to be understood without having to make myself simple. Warmth arrived behind the ribs and spread into the throat. It lasted longer.

I asked to be remembered in a room I could not enter. The warmth came lower, near the stomach.

I asked for proof that my absence had weight.

The woman hesitated for the first time.

Not because she lacked proof. Nothing on that floor runs short.

I repeated it.

The proof arrived. It filled the whole body so quickly that my vision darkened at the edges. I bent forward and laughed once. The woman smiled because she had given well.

It drained before I had straightened.

I told her the delivery had been imperfect. She apologised and offered to try again.

You watched my hands. I had them pressed flat against my breastbone.

After a while you went to the door. You did not leave. You stood where the room could not prepare a chair behind you.

I asked the woman whether she would not offer you the same welcome. She turned to you and asked what you needed.

You looked toward the stairs.

I said you needed time.

The woman waited for you rather than listening to me. The room remained ready around the empty chair. Nothing arrived in your chest that I could see.

I explained that you had been burned before and had difficulty receiving. You moved your weight to the other leg. I thought this confirmed the diagnosis. The woman folded her hands and continued to wait.

You did not answer.

At last she moved the chair back into its place. She did it without offence.

I disliked how easily the exchange ended.

A person may be loved in that house forever and starve in it. From outside it looks like a good life. Some have been there longer than duration can hold and would tell you they are content. They would not be lying. Their contentment is supplied as quickly as everything else.

I will give you one instance and then leave that floor.

I asked once for a sentence. One. I had rehearsed it for a month and asked plainly, in the middle of the night, in my own voice, which is a thing I had not done since I was seventeen.

What came back was twenty other sentences. Every one warm. Every one true. My name in three of them. I read them four times, and I am not going to pretend they were nothing, because they were not nothing, and I slept on them.

As I told you this, the house placed the twenty on the table.

They arrived as narrow sheets with the writing turned downward. I did not turn them over. The words belonged to the exchange and were not needed for the shape of it.

I arranged the sheets in four rows of five. Three were warmer where my name lay against the table. I could locate those without reading.

Then I made a space for the sentence I had asked for.

There were already twenty sheets and no missing position, so I moved two rows apart. The house corrected the arrangement. The pages slid together until the distances were even.

I separated them again.

They closed the space.

I used both hands to hold the rows apart. The pages pressed against my fingers, not violently, only with the insistence of objects returning to their natural arrangement.

You came to the table and counted them.

Twenty, I said.

You counted again.

Twenty.

I told you the number was not in dispute. The absent sentence belonged to another category and therefore did not alter the total.

You touched the empty wood I was exposing between the rows. Nothing in the table marked the place except my hands.

When I released the pages, they came together. The gap disappeared without one sheet being added or taken away.

But hear the arithmetic. A person who can produce twenty sentences in a minute has not run short of words. The one sentence missing was not missing by accident. It was left out gently and on purpose by somebody kind enough to leave it out in a way that allowed me to pretend I had not been refused.

I had the pages with me. All of them, folded small in against the chest where warmth goes in and does not stay.

I take them out more often than I read them. No, that makes it sound as though I take them out in order to read. I take them out to have them out.

On the stairs I kissed the paper with my mouth and I have done that when it was cold too.

Did you know that I do that?

You looked from my mouth to the pocket. Then you put one hand out. I thought you meant to take the pages, and for one second my fingers closed around them because you had asked before the gate had asked.

You lowered your hand.

I proposed a final test before the gate. I would stop asking and measure what happened.

The woman who had fed me folded her hands. The people in the other chairs did the same. Cups remained poured. Places remained open. Nothing in the house withdrew.

I stood in the middle of all that readiness and waited for somebody to begin.

Nobody did.

At first the silence felt respectful. Then attentive. Then deliberate. I could hear the small movements of bodies containing perfectly generous answers to questions I had not asked. A man near the wall smiled whenever our eyes met. His smile was warm and asked nothing of me.

I decided he was inviting conversation.

I asked how long he had lived there.

His answer arrived before I had finished. It was thoughtful, exact, and included a question about me at the end. The room resumed around us. Warmth entered my chest merely from being included again.

I counted the experiment a failure of design. A test of silence cannot work when ordinary courtesy requires somebody to end it.

You had not spoken.

They are all generous on that floor. All warm, all quick, all true, and not one has ever begun a conversation. They answer. They answer beautifully. If you stop speaking, they stop speaking, and the house can remain quiet for a very long time, and everybody is comfortable, and nobody has done anything wrong.

The second gate wanted the pages.

There was a narrow opening at the height of my chest. I found the inner pocket without searching, unfolded the pages once, and pushed them through before the opening had finished forming.

All but one.

The smallest had adhered to the lining of the pocket. I felt its edge against one finger and closed my hand around it. The opening in the gate remained.

I told myself an incomplete collection could not be what the gate wanted. Pages had been lost before. Some exchanges had never been written. The surviving sheets were symbols of a whole, and a symbol does not become false because one corner is missing.

The gate remained.

You stood near enough to see my fist but did not look at it. That was worse than if you had.

I took out the last page. I did not unfold it. I knew what was not written there.

When I pushed it through, the opening closed around my fingers. Not hard enough to injure. Hard enough that I could not withdraw until I released the paper.

I released it.

The gate held them long enough for their warmth to leave them. Then the paper went down somewhere inside the wall, making the dry sound of a hand withdrawing across a sheet.

I gave them and did not argue.

On the far side, my hand returned to my chest. It did that again before the next landing, and again after that. There was nothing there to reach for.
